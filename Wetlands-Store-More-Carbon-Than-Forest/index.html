Wetlands Store More Carbon Than Forests

24 October 2026 Unaisudheen T.P.

When people think about nature helping to fight climate change, forests are usually the first thing that comes to mind. Trees absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and store it in their trunks, branches, and roots. While forests are incredibly important, there is another ecosystem that is even better at storing carbon. That ecosystem is wetlands. Wetlands are among the most valuable ecosystems on Earth. They include marshes, swamps, bogs, mangroves, and peatlands. Although wetlands cover only about three percent of the world's land area, they store nearly thirty percent of the carbon found in the Earth's soil. This is about twice the amount of carbon stored in all the world's forests combined.

Why Are Wetlands So Good at Storing Carbon?

One of the main reasons is the presence of peatlands. Peatlands are a special type of wetland where the soil remains wet for most of the year. In these waterlogged conditions, dead plants do not completely break down.

Instead of decomposing quickly, the plant material slowly builds up over thousands of years, forming a thick layer called peat. Peat is extremely rich in carbon because it stores the carbon that plants absorbed from the atmosphere during their lifetime.

As long as peatlands remain healthy and undisturbed, this carbon stays safely locked in the ground.
